P.O.G. Part 2: Locke & Hobbes and Human Nature[cite: 4]

State of Nature[cite: 4] A hypothetical state where there are no laws, governments, or rules[cite: 10]. People have complete freedom but no guaranteed protection[cite: 9, 10].
Social Contract[cite: 4] An agreement where citizens give up some freedoms in exchange for protection from the government[cite: 8, 9, 10].
Sovereign[cite: 4] A supreme ruler or ultimate authority holding political power[cite: 10].
Tabula Rasa[cite: 4] John Locke's idea that people are born with a "blank slate" and shaped by experiences[cite: 2, 4].
Natural Rights[cite: 4] Fundamental rights all human beings are born with (such as life, liberty, and property)[cite: 2, 4, 9].

1) What were Hobbes' and Locke's beliefs about human nature?[cite: 4]

Hobbes believed people were naturally selfish and chaotic in a state of nature[cite: 4]. Locke believed people were born as a blank slate (Tabula Rasa) and possessed natural rights[cite: 2, 4].

2) Why did they believe government was necessary?[cite: 4]

To prevent chaos and protect people, their rights, and property from others[cite: 4, 9].

3) What did each believe was the purpose/job of government?[cite: 4]

Hobbes felt government needed total control to maintain order[cite: 4, 10]. Locke believed government existed solely to protect natural rights and serve the public[cite: 2, 4].

P.O.G. Part 3: Types of Government[cite: 4]

Autocracy[cite: 10]: One person holds all power (Monarchy or Dictatorship)[cite: 10].
Monarchy[cite: 4, 10]: King/Queen rules through family lineage[cite: 10].
Dictatorship[cite: 4, 10]: Single leader has absolute control over citizens' lives[cite: 10].
Oligarchy[cite: 4, 10]: Small group of people holds all political power[cite: 10].
Theocracy[cite: 4, 10]: Government recognizing God or divine authority as supreme rule[cite: 10].
Anarchy[cite: 4, 10]: Nobody is in control (or everyone is)[cite: 10].
Direct Democracy[cite: 4, 10]: Citizens directly make laws and decisions[cite: 3, 6, 10].
Representative Democracy[cite: 4, 10]: Citizens elect leaders to make laws and govern[cite: 3, 6, 10].

Lesson 3: What is Republican Government?[cite: 3, 7]

Civic Virtue[cite: 3, 7]: Putting aside personal self-interests for the good of the community/country[cite: 2, 5, 6].
Common Good[cite: 2, 3, 7]: Whatever helps the whole community rather than just one person[cite: 2, 5, 7].
Delegate[cite: 3, 7]: A representative chosen by citizens to act on their behalf[cite: 3, 6, 7].
Faction[cite: 3, 7]: Groups of interest that could divide citizens in a large republic[cite: 6].
Republic[cite: 3, 7]: A country where power is held by citizens electing representatives[cite: 6, 7].

How did colonists teach civic virtue?[cite: 5, 8]

Parents taught children at home, teachers taught at school, through stories, and by designing public buildings similarly to ancient Roman buildings[cite: 2, 5].

Example of Civic Virtue: Cincinnatus[cite: 2, 6]

Cincinnatus led Rome to victory in battle, gave up his dictator/military power, and returned to farming[cite: 2, 6].

P.O.G. Part 4: Limiting Government[cite: 3, 9]

Constitution[cite: 3, 9]: Rulebook for government; explains how laws are made and limits government power[cite: 1, 9].
Rule of Law[cite: 1, 3, 9]: Everyone, including government leaders and the president, must follow the law[cite: 1, 9].
Minority Rights[cite: 1, 3, 9]: Protection of small or unpopular groups so the majority cannot treat them unfairly[cite: 1, 9].
Consent of the Governed[cite: 1, 3, 9]: Citizens give permission to be governed by exercising their right to vote[cite: 1, 9].
Separation of Powers[cite: 1, 3, 9]: Dividing government authority among branches (legislative, executive, judicial)[cite: 1, 9].
